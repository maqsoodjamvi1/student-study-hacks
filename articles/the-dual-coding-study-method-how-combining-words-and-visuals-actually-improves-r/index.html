When I went back to school as an adult, I quickly realized that my old college strategy—highlighting textbooks until they looked like abstract art and reading my notes over and over—was completely broken. I would spend three hours staring at a chapter on business logistics, feel like I understood it, and then completely freeze up the moment I saw a quiz question.

The turning point came when I stopped treating my study materials as a wall of text and started building a mental bridge between words and pictures. In cognitive psychology, this is known as dual-coding theory, and learning how to apply it changed my entire approach to studying.

What Dual-Coding Actually Means for Your Brain

At its core, dual-coding is the practice of combining verbal information with visual information. According to the theory behind it, your brain processes information through two separate channels: a verbal channel for text and speech, and a non-verbal channel for images, diagrams, and shapes.

When you only read a textbook, you are putting all the heavy lifting onto the verbal channel. It is like trying to build a house with only a hammer. But when you pair a difficult concept with a rough sketch, a flowchart, or a symbol, you store that information in two different formats in your memory. If you blank on the definition during an exam, the mental image often acts as a backdoor to retrieve the text definition—and vice versa.

The secret is that your visuals do not need to be artistic. Stick figures, messy arrows, and crude box diagrams work just as well as professional illustrations because the act of translating the text into a picture is what forces your brain to truly process what the words mean.

Step-by-Step: How to Build a Dual-Coding Study Session

You do not need an art degree or special software to use this method. A blank piece of paper and a couple of colored pens are enough. Here is how I set up a study session using dual-coding for a dense, concept-heavy topic:

  • Read a single chunk: Read one paragraph or section of your material. Put the highlighter down and close the book so you cannot copy word-for-word.
  • Translate into a concept: Ask yourself, “If I had to explain this process to a ten-year-old using only a drawing on a whiteboard, what would it look like?”
  • Draw the relationship: Sketch the concept. If you are learning how a supply chain works, draw a factory, a delivery truck, and a storefront, then draw arrows connecting them with key terms written above the arrows.
  • Test the map: Cover up your original notes and try to explain the drawing out loud. If you stumble, look at where your sketch failed to capture the details, and fix it.

By forcing yourself to convert sentences into spatial arrangements, you move the information from short-term working memory into long-term storage much faster than passive re-reading ever could.

A Real Example: Studying Abstract Concepts

Let us take a real-world scenario. Imagine you are studying for a certification exam and need to learn how human resource policies handle conflict resolution. A standard textbook gives you four steps in a text paragraph: identification, mediation, resolution, and follow-up.

Instead of writing those words out in a vertical list, you can dual-code them right on your notepad:

  1. Draw a stop sign for identification, and write a quick keyword like “Spot the spark.”
  2. Draw two stick figures shaking hands across a table for mediation, and add a dialogue bubble.
  3. Draw a checkmark inside a shield for resolution.
  4. Draw a small calendar page with a repeating loop arrow for follow-up.

Later, when you are trying to recall the steps under pressure, your mind will naturally picture the stop sign or the calendar page before it retrieves the formal academic definition.

Making Dual-Coding Fit Your Routine

If you study mostly on digital devices, you might be tempted to search Google Images for pre-made diagrams. Resist this urge. Looking at someone else’s finished chart is passive learning, almost identical to reading text. The retention benefit of dual-coding comes entirely from the mental struggle of deciding how to represent the idea yourself.

Start small. Pick just one difficult chapter or module this week and commit to replacing your text-heavy summary notes with crude, hand-drawn concept maps. As you build confidence, you will likely find that your study sessions take less total time because the information sticks on the very first pass.
