When I went back to school in my thirties while working a full-time job, I quickly realized that my old college study habits were completely broken. I could no longer spend four hours rereading a chapter and hoping it stuck. Time was tight, the material for my professional certifications was dense, and I needed a way to actually understand complex concepts on the first pass.

That is when I started using the Feynman Technique. Named after the physicist Richard Feynman, this method is built on a simple premise: if you want to know whether you truly understand something, try explaining it in plain English. Strip away the jargon, drop the textbook definitions, and teach it as if you were talking to a complete beginner.

If you are an adult learner trying to balance career, family, and demanding study goals, this approach cuts your study time in half by exposing what you actually know versus what you are just memorizing. Here is how I put it into practice, step by step.

Step 1: Pick a single, specific concept

The biggest mistake adults make when studying is trying to digest too much at once. When I was studying tax codes, I did not try to learn “deductions” as a broad topic. I focused on one narrow mechanism, like “passive activity loss limits.”

Grab a blank sheet of paper or open a clean document. Write the exact name of the concept at the very top. If your study material is a 30-page chapter, break it down into micro-concepts. You want a topic small enough that you can tackle it in a single sitting without getting overwhelmed by adjacent information.

Step 2: Write out the explanation in plain English

Next, write down an explanation of the concept as if you were teaching it to someone with zero background in the subject. Do not use industry buzzwords, and definitely do not copy sentences verbatim from your study guide or lecture notes.

When I first tried this with complex financial ratios, I caught myself hiding behind terms like “liquidity buffers” and “asset-to-liability ratios.” The trick is to force yourself to use everyday analogies. For instance, instead of defining cash flow theoretically, I compared it to checking my personal household bank account at the end of grocery shopping day.

If you find yourself writing a sentence that sounds like it was lifted directly from a textbook, stop. That is your brain relying on memorization instead of comprehension.

Step 3: Find the gaps where you stutter

Inevitably, as you write or speak your explanation out loud, you will hit a wall. You will find a specific sentence where your logic gets fuzzy, or you will realize you are hand-waving past a crucial step in the process with phrases like “and then magic happens.”

These gaps are gold. They tell you exactly where your understanding breaks down.

When this happens, go back to your source material. Do not reread the whole chapter; just look up the specific missing piece. Read that one section again with laser focus, fix the hole in your explanation, and try writing it out again until the logic flows smoothly from start to finish.

Step 4: Simplify and build analogies

Once you have a complete explanation, review it one more time with an eye toward radical simplicity. Can you cut the word count in half? Can you replace a complicated paragraph with a solid, relatable analogy?

Here is what this looks like in practice:

  • Complex definition: “Asymmetric encryption utilizes a public and private key pair to ensure confidentiality and non-repudiation across unsecured networks.”
  • Feynman translation: “Think of it like a padlock. Anyone in the world can snap the open padlock onto a box to lock it (the public key), but only you have the physical key to unlock it later (the private key).”

When you can map a technical concept to a physical object or a common everyday experience, your brain anchors the information permanently. You no longer have to strain to recall the definition during an exam or a high-stakes work meeting.

Making it work with a busy schedule

As an adult, you do not have hours to waste on elaborate study systems that require color-coded flashcards or mind-mapping software. The beauty of this technique is its portability. You do not even need a pen and paper once you get the hang of it; you can practice it silently while walking the dog or commuting to work.

Try taking the hardest concept from your current study session and running it through these four steps today. You will quickly figure out the difference between recognizing information on a page and actually owning the knowledge.
